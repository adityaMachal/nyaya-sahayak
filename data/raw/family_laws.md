# Family Laws of India

Covers Hindu Marriage Act 1955 (HMA), Special Marriage Act 1954 (SMA), maintenance provisions under CrPC/BNSS, and the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005.

Source: India Code (https://www.indiacode.nic.in)

# Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 codifies the law relating to marriage among Hindus. It applies to any person who is a Hindu by religion (including Virashaiva, Lingayat, or follower of the Brahmo, Prarthana, or Arya Samaj), as well as to Buddhists, Jains, and Sikhs. The Act lays down conditions for a valid marriage, provisions for restitution of conjugal rights, judicial separation, and divorce.

## Section 5: Conditions for a Hindu Marriage

A marriage may be solemnized between any two Hindus if the following conditions are fulfilled: (i) neither party has a spouse living at the time of the marriage; (ii) at the time of marriage, neither party is incapable of giving valid consent due to unsoundness of mind, or even if capable of giving consent, has been suffering from a mental disorder of such kind or to such extent as to be unfit for marriage and procreation; (iii) the bridegroom has completed the age of twenty-one years and the bride has completed the age of eighteen years at the time of the marriage; (iv) the parties are not within the degrees of prohibited relationship unless the custom or usage governing each of them permits a marriage between the two; (v) the parties are not sapindas of each other, unless the custom or usage governing each of them permits such a marriage.

A marriage solemnized in contravention of conditions (i), (iv), or (v) is void under Section 11. A marriage in contravention of condition (ii) is voidable under Section 12. Contravention of the age condition (iii) does not render the marriage void or voidable, but the parties may be punished under the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006.

## Section 7: Ceremonies for a Hindu Marriage

A Hindu marriage may be solemnized in accordance with the customary rites and ceremonies of either party thereto. Where such rites and ceremonies include the saptapadi — that is, the taking of seven steps by the bridegroom and the bride jointly before the sacred fire — the marriage becomes complete and binding when the seventh step is taken. The Act recognizes customary diversity and does not prescribe a single mandatory ceremony; the key requirement is that the marriage is performed in accordance with recognized rites of at least one of the parties.

## Section 8: Registration of Hindu Marriages

The State Government may make rules for the registration of Hindu marriages for the purpose of facilitating proof of Hindu marriages. Registration is effected by entering prescribed particulars in a Hindu Marriage Register maintained by the district or area as determined by the State Government. While registration provides strong evidentiary value, the Supreme Court has directed all states to make registration of marriages compulsory (Seema v. Ashwani Kumar, 2006). Non-registration does not affect the validity of the marriage, but failure to register may be penalized with a fine.

## Section 9: Restitution of Conjugal Rights

When either the husband or the wife has, without reasonable excuse, withdrawn from the society of the other, the aggrieved party may apply by petition to the district court for restitution of conjugal rights. The court, on being satisfied of the truth of the statements made in the petition and that there is no legal ground why the application should not be granted, may decree restitution of conjugal rights. The burden of proving reasonable excuse lies on the party who has withdrawn from the society of the other. This provision has been debated on constitutional grounds, though the Supreme Court upheld it in Saroj Rani v. Sudarshan Kumar (1984).

## Section 10: Judicial Separation

Either party to a marriage may present a petition for judicial separation on any of the grounds specified in Section 13, and in the case of the wife, also on the grounds specified in Section 13(2). A decree for judicial separation does not dissolve the marriage, but the petitioner is no longer obliged to cohabit with the respondent. The court may rescind the decree of judicial separation on the application of either party if it considers it just and reasonable to do so. If there is no resumption of cohabitation for a period of one year or more after passing of such decree, either party may apply for divorce on that ground.

## Section 13: Grounds for Divorce

Any marriage solemnized under this Act may be dissolved by a decree of divorce on a petition presented by either party on the following grounds:

1. **Adultery** — The other party has, after the solemnization of marriage, had voluntary sexual intercourse with any person other than his or her spouse.
2. **Cruelty** — The other party has, after the solemnization of marriage, treated the petitioner with cruelty. Cruelty includes both physical and mental cruelty. Mental cruelty can consist of persistent denial of conjugal rights, false accusations of adultery, or persistent taunting.
3. **Desertion** — The other party has deserted the petitioner for a continuous period of not less than two years immediately preceding the presentation of the petition. Desertion implies both factum of separation and animus deserendi (intention to desert).
4. **Conversion** — The other party has ceased to be a Hindu by conversion to another religion.
5. **Unsoundness of Mind** — The other party has been incurably of unsound mind, or has been suffering continuously or intermittently from mental disorder of such a kind and to such an extent that the petitioner cannot reasonably be expected to live with the respondent.
6. **Venereal Disease** — The other party has been suffering from a virulent form of leprosy or venereal disease in a communicable form.
7. **Renunciation** — The other party has renounced the world by entering any religious order.
8. **Presumption of Death** — The other party has not been heard of as being alive for a period of seven years or more by those persons who would naturally have heard of it, had that party been alive.

Additionally, a wife may also present a petition on the ground that the husband has been guilty of rape, sodomy, or bestiality, or that a marriage was solemnized before she attained the age of fifteen years and she has repudiated the marriage before attaining eighteen years.

## Section 13B: Divorce by Mutual Consent

A petition for dissolution of marriage by mutual consent may be presented to the district court by both parties to a marriage together on the ground that they have been living separately for a period of one year or more, that they have not been able to live together, and that they have mutually agreed that the marriage should be dissolved. On the motion of both parties made not earlier than six months after the date of presentation of the petition and not later than eighteen months after the said date, the court shall pass a decree of divorce if it is satisfied that the marriage has been solemnized and that the averments in the petition are true.

The Supreme Court, in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur (2017), held that the six-month cooling-off period prescribed under Section 13B(2) is not mandatory and can be waived by the court in appropriate cases where it is satisfied that there is no possibility of reconciliation. This was a significant departure from the earlier position and has been applied by courts to expedite mutual consent divorces where parties have already been separated for a long time.

## Section 24: Maintenance Pendente Lite and Expenses of Proceedings

Where in any proceeding under this Act it appears to the court that either the wife or the husband, as the case may be, has no independent income sufficient for her or his support and the necessary expenses of the proceeding, the court may, on the application of the wife or husband, order the respondent to pay the petitioner the expenses of the proceeding, and monthly during the proceeding such sum as, having regard to the petitioner's own income and the income of the respondent, it may seem to the court to be reasonable. The payment of maintenance pendente lite is meant to ensure that a needy spouse is not disadvantaged in pursuing or defending matrimonial proceedings due to lack of resources.

## Section 25: Permanent Alimony and Maintenance

Any court exercising jurisdiction under this Act may, at the time of passing any decree or at any time subsequent thereto, on application made by either the wife or the husband, order that the respondent shall pay to the applicant for her or his maintenance and support such gross sum or such monthly or periodical sum for a term not exceeding the life of the applicant as, having regard to the respondent's own income and other property, the income and other property of the applicant, the conduct of the parties, and other circumstances of the case, it may seem to the court to be just. The court may also modify or rescind the order if circumstances change.

## Section 26: Custody of Children

In any proceeding under this Act, the court may, from time to time, pass such interim orders and make such provisions in the decree as it may deem just and proper with respect to the custody, maintenance, and education of minor children, consistently with their wishes and welfare. The paramount consideration in deciding custody is the welfare of the child. The court may also issue appropriate directions for the visitation rights of the non-custodial parent.

# Special Marriage Act, 1954

The Special Marriage Act, 1954 provides a form of civil marriage for citizens of India irrespective of the religion or faith followed by either party. It is particularly significant for inter-religious and inter-caste marriages, as it does not require either party to convert to the other's religion. The marriage may be solemnized without any religious ceremony.

## Key Provisions of the Special Marriage Act

**Eligibility:** The male must have completed 21 years and the female 18 years of age. Neither party should have a living spouse, and both must be capable of giving valid consent. The parties must not be within degrees of prohibited relationship unless permitted by custom.

**Notice of Intended Marriage (Section 5-8):** The parties intending to marry must give notice in writing to the Marriage Officer of the district in which at least one of the parties has resided for at least 30 days immediately preceding the date of notice. The notice is entered in the Marriage Notice Book and published by affixing it at a conspicuous place in the office. Any person may, within 30 days from the date of publication, object to the marriage on the ground that it would contravene a condition specified in Section 4. If no objection is received, or if objections are overruled, the Marriage Officer solemnizes the marriage.

**Solemnization (Section 12):** The marriage is solemnized in the presence of the Marriage Officer and three witnesses. No religious ceremony is required. Both parties declare in a prescribed form that they take each other as lawful spouses. The marriage is then registered in the Marriage Certificate Book.

**Divorce:** Divorce under the Special Marriage Act is available on grounds similar to those under the Hindu Marriage Act including adultery, cruelty, desertion for two years, and mutual consent.

# Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS: Maintenance of Wife, Children, and Parents

## Section 125 CrPC (now Section 144 BNSS): Order for Maintenance

If any person having sufficient means neglects or refuses to maintain: (a) his wife, unable to maintain herself; (b) his legitimate or illegitimate minor child, whether married or not, unable to maintain itself; (c) his legitimate or illegitimate child (not being a married daughter) who has attained majority, where such child is, by reason of any physical or mental abnormality or injury, unable to maintain itself; or (d) his father or mother, unable to maintain himself or herself — a Magistrate of the first class may, upon proof of such neglect or refusal, order such person to make a monthly allowance for the maintenance of his wife, child, father, or mother, at such monthly rate as the Magistrate thinks fit.

The maximum maintenance that can be awarded under this provision was previously capped at ₹500 per month but now has no upper limit. The "wife" includes a woman who has been divorced by or has obtained a divorce from her husband and has not remarried. However, a wife is not entitled to maintenance if she is living in adultery, or if without sufficient reason she refuses to live with her husband, or if they are living separately by mutual consent. These provisions apply to all persons irrespective of religion, making Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS a secular remedy available to women of all faiths.

# Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 (DV Act) provides for more effective protection of the rights of women guaranteed under the Constitution who are victims of violence of any kind occurring within the family. It is a civil law designed primarily for protection and not punishment, though breach of protection orders is a criminal offence.

## Key Provisions of the Domestic Violence Act

**Definition of Domestic Violence (Section 3):** Domestic violence includes any act, omission, commission, or conduct that harms or injures or endangers the health, safety, life, limb, or well-being — whether mental or physical — of the aggrieved person. It includes physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse, and economic abuse. Economic abuse includes deprivation of all or any economic or financial resources, disposal of household effects, or prohibition from accessing resources she is entitled to use.

**Protection Orders (Section 18):** The Magistrate may pass a protection order prohibiting the respondent from committing any act of domestic violence, aiding or abetting in the commission of acts of domestic violence, entering the aggrieved person's place of employment, or attempting to communicate with the aggrieved person. Breach of a protection order is a cognizable and non-bailable offence punishable with imprisonment up to one year, or with fine up to ₹20,000, or both.

**Residence Orders (Section 19):** The Magistrate may pass a residence order restraining the respondent from dispossessing or disturbing the aggrieved person's possession of the shared household, directing the respondent to remove himself from the shared household, or directing the respondent to secure alternate accommodation for the aggrieved person. The aggrieved person has a right to reside in the shared household whether or not she has any title or rights in the shared household.

**Monetary Relief (Section 20):** The Magistrate may direct the respondent to pay monetary relief to meet expenses incurred and losses suffered by the aggrieved person and any child of the aggrieved person as a result of domestic violence. This includes the loss of earnings, medical expenses, loss caused due to destruction, damage, or removal of property from the control of the aggrieved person, and maintenance for the aggrieved person as well as her children.

**Compensation Orders (Section 22):** The Magistrate may in addition to other reliefs grant compensation and damages for injuries, including mental torture and emotional distress, caused by the acts of domestic violence committed by the respondent.

**Right to File Complaint:** An aggrieved person, a Protection Officer, or any other person on behalf of the aggrieved person may file a complaint. The complaint can be filed before the Magistrate. The court should dispose of every application within 60 days of the first hearing.
